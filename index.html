The name

Brahmaputra + Moskva

Two rivers, two countries. BrahMos Aerospace is a joint venture of India's DRDO and Russia's NPO Mashinostroyeniya, set up by an agreement signed on 12 February 1998. The missile grew out of the Russian P-800 Oniks.

Anatomy

Seven sections. Two engines.

A rocket to get it moving and a ramjet to keep it fast. Everything else is there to find a target and hit it.

Assembled

Three tonnes in a sealed tube.

It is stored, carried and fired from its own canister. Keep scrolling to watch it launch.

01Nose · centre body

Active radar seeker

The cone in the middle of the nose holds the missile's own radar. It stays off for most of the flight and turns on near the end, when the missile has to find its target.

  1. Wakes in the final phase of flight
  2. Searches the area ahead and picks out its target, even in a group of ships
  3. Locks on and steers the missile all the way in
  • Active radar homing
  • Indian seeker flew 22 Mar 2018
  • Picked one target from a group in 2009
02Nose · annular inlet

Air intake

A ramjet has no fan and no compressor. The missile's speed does that job: air rushes into the ring around the cone, and shock waves off the cone slow it down and squeeze it before it reaches the engine.

  1. Air enters the ring around the cone at around Mach 2.8
  2. Shock waves off the cone slow it down and pack it tighter
  3. Hot, dense air travels back to the combustor
  • No moving parts
  • Only works at supersonic speed
  • That's why it needs a booster
03Forward bay

Guidance & navigation

Gyroscopes and accelerometers (an inertial navigation system) track every turn the missile makes. Satellite fixes stop that estimate from drifting, so it stays on course over hundreds of kilometres.

  1. Inertial navigation tracks position from the moment of launch
  2. GPS, GLONASS and GAGAN correct it along the way, using India's 17 g G3OM receiver chip
  3. Handover to the radar seeker near the target
  • INS + multi-GNSS
  • Reported accuracy ≈ 1 m CEP
04Mid-forward body

Warhead

A conventional semi-armour-piercing warhead, 200 kg on ship and land versions and up to 300 kg on the air-launched one. It is built to punch through first and explode inside.

  1. Hits at nearly three times the speed of sound
  2. Breaks through a hull or a hardened roof
  3. Detonates inside the target
  • 200–300 kg SAP
  • Impact energy grows with speed²
  • ≈ 9× a subsonic missile of the same mass
05Centre body

Fuel tank

The longest section. It carries liquid fuel for the ramjet. That is the key to range: the ramjet takes oxygen from the air, so the tank only has to hold fuel.

  1. Feeds the ramjet for the whole cruise
  2. Made in India since DRDO's DMSRDE lab replaced the imported Russian fuel
  3. Stays liquid in deep cold at high altitude
  • Liquid ramjet fuel
  • No oxidiser on board
06Mid-body & tail

Wings & control fins

Four wings in the middle give lift. Four fins at the tail are moved by actuators to steer. Together they let a missile at Mach 2.8 swerve and then dive steeply.

  1. Wings hold it up in thin air at high altitude
  2. Tail fins pitch, yaw and roll it
  3. S-turns near the target make it hard to intercept
  • Four wings, four fins in an X
  • "S" manoeuvre at Mach 2.8
  • Steep dive for land targets
07Aft body · stage two

Liquid-fuel ramjet

This engine does most of the work. Squeezed air from the intake mixes with fuel, burns in the combustion chamber and blasts out of the nozzle. It holds the missile near Mach 2.8 for the whole cruise.

  1. Air arrives from the intake already compressed
  2. Fuel is sprayed in and burned behind flame holders
  3. Exhaust leaves the nozzle and pushes the missile
  • Air-breathing
  • More fuel-efficient than a rocket
  • Design from NPO Mashinostroyeniya
08Tail · stage one

Solid rocket booster

A ramjet can't start from a standstill, so a solid rocket fires first. It sits inside the ramjet's empty combustion chamber, a layout taken from the P-800 Oniks. This keeps the missile short.

  1. Fires and lifts the missile out of its canister
  2. Accelerates it to supersonic speed (around Mach 2)
  3. Burns out and is pushed out of the tail, leaving the chamber free for the ramjet
  • Solid propellant
  • Smaller booster on BrahMos-A
  • Drops away after burnout

Flight

From canister to target

T−0

Sealed in a canister

The missile lives in a sealed transport-launch canister. It can fire from a ship's vertical launcher, a 12×12 truck carrying three canisters, a Su-30MKI fighter or a submarine.

Stage 1

Booster ignites

The solid rocket fires. The missile leaves the canister, then tips over toward the target as it speeds up.

Stage 1 → 2

Booster drops off, ramjet starts

Once it is supersonic, the spent booster is pushed out of the tail and falls away. Air is now coming into the intake fast enough to run the ramjet, and it lights.

Cruise

Fast and high

It climbs as high as 15 km, where the thin air means less drag and more range, and holds about Mach 2.8. It can also fly the whole way low, skimming the sea.

Terminal

Down to the waves

Near the target it drops to as low as 3–10 m above the water and turns its radar seeker on. That low, it stays below the horizon of the target's radar until it is very close.

Impact

S-turn and strike

A weaving "S" manoeuvre at Mach 2.8 makes it harder to shoot down. Against land targets it can dive steeply instead. Defenders have seconds to react.

One missile, every platform

Land, sea, air and under water

01

Ship-launched

In service

Fires from vertical launch cells or angled launchers. It equips about 20 frontline Indian Navy warships. The first vertical launch was from INS Ranvir in 2008.

02

Land-based

In service

Mobile Autonomous Launchers on 12×12 trucks. The Indian Army raised its first BrahMos regiment in 2007, and newer blocks add land attack and steep dives.

03

BrahMos-A · air-launched

In service

Carried under the Su-30MKI. A lighter build (about 2.5 t) with a smaller booster. First fired from the air on 22 November 2017.

04

Submarine-launched

Tested

First fired on 20 March 2013 from a pontoon submerged 40–50 m deep off Visakhapatnam. It can strike both ships and land targets.

05

BrahMos-NG

In development

"Next Generation": about 6 m long and 1.2–1.5 t, so smaller fighters and ships can carry it. Planned with an AESA radar seeker.

06

BrahMos-II

Future

A planned hypersonic successor with a scramjet, aiming well beyond Mach 5. It is still in development.

Timeline

From a handshake to the front line

  1. 1998

    India and Russia sign the agreement that creates BrahMos Aerospace (12 Feb).

  2. 2001

    First test firing from Chandipur, Odisha (12 Jun).

  3. 2007

    The Indian Army raises its first BrahMos regiment.

  4. 2008

    First land-attack test from INS Rajput, then the first vertical launch from INS Ranvir.

  5. 2013

    First launch from under water.

  6. 2016

    India joins the MTCR, so the range can go past 290 km.

  7. 2017

    First extended-range test (11 Mar). First air launch from a Su-30MKI (22 Nov).

  8. 2018

    Indian-built radar seeker flies for the first time.

  9. 2022

    The Philippines signs the first export deal, for about US$375 M.

  10. 2024

    The first export battery is delivered to the Philippines.

  11. 2025

    Reported first combat use, in the May India–Pakistan conflict. A new production site opens in Lucknow.

  12. 2027

    Target date for the 800 km version, now in trials.
